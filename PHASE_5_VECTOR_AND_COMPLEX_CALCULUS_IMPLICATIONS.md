# Phase 5 vector and complex-calculus implications

## 1. Verdict

The one-phase Euler representation gives a rigorous geometric coordinate for
an eligible one-dimensional diffusion. It does **not** create a new vector
dimension, a new stochastic driver, or a holomorphic calculus. Its real value
is more specific:

1. it identifies the exact curve on which the complex state lives;
2. it gives tangent, normal, metric, and transported-group formulas in one
   notation;
3. it supplies bounded phase coordinates for an unbounded scalar state;
4. it exposes which invariants a nonlinear transform can preserve; and
5. it makes rank and topology obstructions unusually transparent.

These are useful geometric tools for stochastic calculus on a curve. They are
not a replacement for multidimensional Itô calculus, differential geometry,
Wirtinger calculus, or numerical analysis.

The mathematical claims in this report are proved in
[PHASE_5_MATHEMATICAL_FOUNDATIONS.md](PHASE_5_MATHEMATICAL_FOUNDATIONS.md)
and
[PHASE_5_RIGIDITY_AND_GROUP_STRUCTURE.md](PHASE_5_RIGIDITY_AND_GROUP_STRUCTURE.md).
The computational controls are preserved in
[notebooks/phase5_one_phase_euler.ipynb](notebooks/phase5_one_phase_euler.ipynb).

## 2. What the complex plane contributes

Let

\[
Z_t=Z_0F(\theta_t),
\qquad
F(\theta)=r(\theta)e^{i\theta},
\]

where \(F:I\to\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}\) is \(C^2\) and injective. Under the
identification

\[
\mathbb C\cong\mathbb R^2,\qquad x+iy\longleftrightarrow(x,y),
\]

the derivative \(F'(\theta)\) is the tangent vector and \(iF'(\theta)\) is a
normal vector. The speed and signed curvature are

\[
v(\theta)=|F'(\theta)|,
\qquad
\kappa(\theta)
=
\frac{\operatorname{Im}\!\left(\overline{F'(\theta)}F''(\theta)\right)}
{|F'(\theta)|^3}.
\]

If

\[
d\theta_t=m(\theta_t)\,dt+s(\theta_t)\,dW_t,
\]

then Itô's formula gives

\[
dZ_t
=Z_0\left[
F'm+\frac12F''s^2
\right]dt
+Z_0F's\,dW_t.
\]

This is a compact complex form of an ordinary two-component Itô equation.
The instantaneous noise covariance in real coordinates is

\[
\Sigma(\theta)
=s(\theta)^2
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorname{Re}(Z_0F')\\
\operatorname{Im}(Z_0F')
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
\operatorname{Re}(Z_0F')&
\operatorname{Im}(Z_0F')
\end{pmatrix},
\]

an outer product of rank at most one. Multiplication by \(i\) rotates the
tangent by \(90^\circ\); it does not provide an independent random
increment.

### 2.1 The canonical secant curve is actually a line

For the canonical representation,

\[
F(\theta)=\sec\theta\,e^{i\theta}
=1+i\tan\theta,
\qquad
-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}.
\]

Hence

\[
F'(\theta)=i\sec^2\theta,\qquad
F''(\theta)=2i\sec^2\theta\tan\theta,
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Im}(\overline{F'}F'')=0.
\]

Its curvature is exactly zero. The phase changes the speed at which the
vertical affine line is traversed; it does not bend the original state into
a rotating trajectory. The notebook returned a maximum absolute numerical
curvature of exactly zero in
[vector_geometry_summary.json](phase5_evidence/vector_geometry_summary.json).

This resolves an important interpretive point: the factors
\(\sec\theta\) and \(e^{i\theta}\) rotate and rescale algebraically, but their
product remains on a straight line.

## 3. Dimension and rank

### 3.1 One phase is one state coordinate

An injective phase map is a diffeomorphic reparameterization of a
one-dimensional state. Consequently,

\[
\sigma(\theta_t)=\sigma(X_t)
\]

for the canonical exact map \(X_t-X_0=c\tan\theta_t\), and the filtrations
generated by the complete paths agree after augmentation:

\[
\mathcal F_t^\theta=\mathcal F_t^X.
\]

No information is lost, but no new information is created.

### 3.2 Instantaneous rank is not the same as terminal support rank

The notebook includes two necessary negative controls:

- planar Brownian motion has a rank-two terminal covariance and cannot be
  losslessly encoded by one smooth phase at a fixed time;
- the one-driver hypoelliptic system

  \[
  dX_t=dW_t,\qquad dY_t=X_t\,dt
  \]

  has only one instantaneous driver but a full-rank terminal covariance

  \[
  \operatorname{Cov}(X_1,Y_1)
  =
  \begin{pmatrix}
  1&1/2\\
  1/2&1/3
  \end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \det=\frac1{12}>0.
  \]

The empirical covariance differed from this matrix by at most
\(4.18\times10^{-3}\). See
[vector_rank_controls.csv](phase5_evidence/vector_rank_controls.csv).

Therefore neither of the following shortcuts is valid:

\[
\text{one Brownian driver}\Longrightarrow
\text{one-dimensional terminal support},
\]

or

\[
\text{complex notation}\Longrightarrow
\text{two stochastic dimensions}.
\]

The correct object is the rank and propagation of the diffusion distribution,
including its interaction with drift.

## 4. What vector geometry is genuinely useful

### 4.1 Tangent-normal bookkeeping

Complex multiplication packages planar rotations efficiently. For a
curve-valued diffusion, \(F'\) and \(iF'\) give a concise moving frame.
This can simplify:

- projection of vector fields onto a state curve;
- separation of tangential stochastic forcing from normal constraint error;
- curvature terms produced by Itô's formula;
- checks that a numerical method remains on a prescribed manifold; and
- visualization of state-dependent diffusion directions.

For the Phase 5 affine process the curve is a line, so this machinery reduces
to elementary one-dimensional calculus. It becomes more substantive for
nonlinear constrained state spaces, but such systems require a separate
model and cannot be inferred from the affine example.

### 4.2 Rigidity becomes a curve-classification problem

The diffusion coefficient in

\[
dZ=A\,dt+B\,dW
\]

forces \(F's\) to be constant, hence the admissible curve is a line.
For

\[
\frac{dZ}{Z}=A\,dt+B\,dW,
\]

the relative tangent \(F'/F\) is forced to be constant, hence the admissible
curve is a logarithmic spiral. The drift equation imposes an additional
compatibility condition in both cases.

This line-versus-spiral classification is a useful geometric synthesis:
additive noise requires constant tangent, while multiplicative noise requires
constant logarithmic tangent. It does not create a new stochastic calculus;
it translates coefficient matching into differential geometry.

### 4.3 Transported group operations

The map \(x=c\tan\theta\) transports real addition to

\[
\theta_1\oplus\theta_2
=
\arctan\!\bigl(\tan\theta_1+\tan\theta_2\bigr).
\]

This is an exact abelian group law on
\((-\pi/2,\pi/2)\), with identity \(0\) and inverse \(-\theta\). The notebook
verified the tangent-addition identity to \(3.11\times10^{-15}\) and
associativity to \(1.53\times10^{-15}\), while ordinary angular addition
differed by an RMS value of \(0.288\). See
[transported_group_checks.csv](phase5_evidence/transported_group_checks.csv).

This operation is useful when calculations must remain in the compact phase
chart. It is not ordinary rotation or complex multiplication. Its apparent
nonlinearity is exactly real addition conjugated by a coordinate map.

### 4.4 Invariant-preserving transforms

The Cayley variable

\[
U=\frac{1+iY/c}{1-iY/c}=e^{i\Theta}
\]

has \(|U|=1\) exactly and is losslessly invertible away from the inaccessible
point \(-1\). This genuinely supplies a unit-modulus invariant for the
**transformed** state. It does not show that the original additive state has
constant radius, and its state-dependent angular diffusion is not circular
Brownian motion.

The notebook found a maximum modulus defect of
\(3.33\times10^{-16}\) for the exact transform, compared with \(0.718\) for
an unconstrained complex Euler step on the same sample path. See
[cayley_invariants.csv](phase5_evidence/cayley_invariants.csv). The result is
a concrete geometric-numerical benefit of using the exact map, not a claim
that all complex reformulations preserve invariants.

## 5. Complex differentiation

### 5.1 A curve does not determine a complex derivative

A complex derivative is defined through limits from every direction in an
open subset of \(\mathbb C\). A payoff known only on the one-dimensional
curve \(F(I)\) determines its tangential derivative but not its normal
derivative.

For the canonical vertical line \(F(I)=\{1+iy:y\in\mathbb R\}\), consider

\[
H_0(z)=0,
\qquad
H_1(z)=\operatorname{Re}z-1.
\]

Both restrict to zero on the entire state line, yet their derivatives in the
real normal direction are \(0\) and \(1\). Thus the stochastic state data do
not select a unique off-curve extension, much less a unique holomorphic one.
The notebook verifies exact agreement on the curve while recording those two
different normal derivatives in
[complex_derivative_summary.json](phase5_evidence/complex_derivative_summary.json).

### 5.2 Wirtinger calculus is valid but not newly unlocked

For a \(C^1\), possibly nonholomorphic function \(H(z,\bar z)\),

\[
dH
=H_z\,dZ+H_{\bar z}\,d\bar Z
\]

at first order, with the appropriate quadratic terms in Itô calculus. Along a
curve \(z=F(\theta)\),

\[
\frac{d}{d\theta}H(F(\theta),\overline{F(\theta)})
=H_zF'+H_{\bar z}\overline{F'}.
\]

The notebook checked this identity for \(H(z,\bar z)=|z|^2\) with maximum
error \(3.55\times10^{-15}\). This is a concise notation for two real
derivatives. Unless \(H_{\bar z}=0\), it is not holomorphic calculus.

### 5.3 Complex-step differentiation is a separate method

For a real function with an analytic extension,

\[
f'(x)\approx\frac{\operatorname{Im}f(x+ih)}{h}
\]

avoids the subtraction in a centered finite difference. The notebook's
\(f(x)=e^x\) benchmark reproduces its known numerical stability at small
\(h\); see
[complex_step_benchmark.csv](phase5_evidence/complex_step_benchmark.csv).

That advantage comes from analyticity of the chosen extension and the
complex-step algorithm. The one-phase Brownian embedding neither guarantees
analyticity of a payoff nor supplies its off-curve values. The two ideas
should therefore not be conflated.

## 6. Does the representation unlock vector stochastic calculus?

Under the acceptance standard in the Phase 5 plan, the answer is:

\[
\boxed{\text{No new vector stochastic calculus is unlocked.}}
\]

The representation does, however, give a useful **coordinate interface** to
existing vector and geometric tools. The distinction is:

| Question | Phase 5 answer |
|---|---|
| Does one phase create an independent planar noise direction? | No. |
| Does \(i\) rotate tangent and normal vectors compactly? | Yes. |
| Does the secant representation curve the additive path? | No; it is a line. |
| Can a transformed state preserve unit modulus exactly? | Yes; the Cayley transform does. |
| Does one driver force every terminal law to have rank one? | No; the hypoelliptic control is full rank. |
| Does a complex embedding make payoffs holomorphic? | No. |
| Can Wirtinger notation organize nonholomorphic derivatives? | Yes, as an equivalent two-real-variable calculus. |
| Does the phase group law replace real addition by genuine rotation? | No; it is conjugated real addition. |

## 7. Research directions that remain legitimate

The following extensions may be worth separate study:

1. invariant-preserving integrators for genuinely curved complex manifolds;
2. optimal compactification maps for specific unbounded-domain generators;
3. phase-amplitude coordinates for systems with actual rotational drift;
4. Lie-bracket and Hörmander analysis in complex notation for
   hypoelliptic systems;
5. holomorphic semigroups when the model and payoff truly satisfy analytic
   hypotheses; and
6. whether a chosen curve coordinate improves conditioning, variance, or
   boundary treatment in a specified application.

Each requires its own theorem or matched benchmark. None follows merely from
writing \(Z=r(\theta)e^{i\theta}\).

## 8. Final implication statement

The one-phase Euler form is best understood as an exact, lossless geometric
chart for a one-dimensional stochastic state. It imports the language of
curves, tangents, rotations, compactification, and transported groups into
the discussion. That language is useful and sometimes numerically practical,
but the underlying stochastic calculus remains one-dimensional until the
model itself supplies additional state directions or drivers.
